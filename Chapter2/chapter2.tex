\section{Exploring Neurons in the Electron Microscopy Volume}
    \subsection{Connectome data}
    The connectome of a 2-hour old first instar larval brain was used, as reconstructed previously by us \citep{winding2023connectome}. The neuronal reconstructions, synapse labels, and neuron annotations, together with the electron microscopy volume, is available at the \href{https://l1em.catmaid.virtualflybrain.org/?pid=1&zp=108250&yp=82961.59999999999&xp=54210.799999999996&tool=tracingtool&sid0=1&s0=2.4999999999999996&help=true&layout=h(XY,%20%7B%20type:%20%22neuron-search%22,%20id:%20%22neuron-search-1%22,%20options:%20%7B%22annotation-name%22:%20%22papers%22%7D%7D,%200.6)}{VirtualFlyBrain}
  
    \subsection{Manual Segmentation}
    Neurons were manually segmented in the EM volume of the first instar larval brain using the web-based software CATMAID (Collaborative Annotation Toolkit for Massive Amounts of Image Data) \citep{schneider2016quantitative, saalfeld2009catmaid}. 

    The initial reconstructions of some central complex neurons and their downstream partners were incomplete, with missing processes and synapses. For these neurons, I performed complete skeleton tracing to capture their full morphology, and systematically annotated all pre- and post-synaptic sites. This ensured comprehensive coverage of their connectivity patterns.
    
    \subsection{Circuit Analysis}
    Circuits were analysed using several tools available in CATMAID. \citep{schneider2016quantitative, saalfeld2009catmaid}. I used the connectivity widget to explore immediate upstream and downstream partners, and the connectivity matrix widget to understand relationship between pairs of selected neuron groups. The connectivity matrix widget generates directed connectivity matrices between identified central complex neuropils. For each pair of neuropils i, j (or each pair of neuron types), I computed an adjacency entry A{ij} equal to the total number of presynaptic–postsynaptic contacts from neurons with arbours in neuropil i onto neurons with arbours in neuropil j. The matrix was often split in axo-axonic or axo dendritic synapses for further analysis. 
    
    Finally, I used the graph widget to visualise networks of interest in the form of comprehensive graphs with neurons represented by nodes and synapses represented by directional edges.

   \subsection{Creating 3D volumes for CX Neuropils}
    Neurons were reconstructed in three dimensions using the CATMAID software. In this process, I focused on the areas circumscribing specific neurons, as well as the regions surrounding input and output synapses. For each CX neuropil, I identified the areas with the highest concentration of synapses to define the boundaries for the 3D volumes. This approach allowed me to create detailed 3D representations of the neuronal structures, which are illustrated in figure \ref{cx_ortographic}. Additionally, I utilized these volumes to generate a cartoon diagram, presented in figure \ref{cxdiagram}.

\section{Nomenclature}
 \label{nomenclature}
 Neurons were classified using the suffixes ".b" (axon boutons) and ".d" (dendrites) for CX neuropils.
Hence, a neuron annotated as NO.b projects its axon into the NO, and likewise, a neuron annotated as FB.d places its dendrites in the FB.
Neurons that are both .b and .d for CX neuropils were annotated as Core CX neurons.

\section{Identifying non-Mushroom Body Dopaminergic Neurons}
    \subsection{Multicolour Stochastic Labelling via FLP-out} 
    I WAS interested if any of the identified central complex neurons are dopaminergic. To verify that, I used the GAL4/UAS system to tag all the dopaminergic neurons(DANs) in the brain, and focused on those located outside the Mushroom Body (non-MB DANs).

    The TH-GAL4 driver line used in this study expresses GAL4 specifically in dopaminergic neurons (DANs) by utilizing the tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) promoter. Tyrosine hydroxylase is an enzyme required for dopamine synthesis and therefore serves as a reliable marker for dopaminergic neurons. By placing the GAL4 gene under control of the TH promoter, GAL4 expression is restricted to TH-positive neurons, which are exclusively dopaminergic.

    To achieve stochastic multicolour labeling of individual neurons, I used the Multicolour Flp\-Out (MCFO) system, which relies on FLP recombinase-mediated excision of FPL Recombination Target sites (FRT) present around transcriptional stop cassettes placed upstream of multiple fluorescent reporter genes. 
    The reporter used here is known as 'spaghetti monster' GFP, or smGFP, which is modified a GFP made of : a superfolder green fluorescent protein (sfGFP, non fluorescent) which has added to it an N-terminal myristoylation signal sequence, and finally attached to it are 10 tandem repeats of a single epitope tag such as HA, FLAG or V5 (see figure \ref{MCFOsystem}).
    
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=12cm]{Figs/CX/FLPGenetics.pdf}
        \caption[Multicolour Flp\-Out (MCFO) system]{Multicolour Flp\-Out (MCFO) system; image adapted from \citep{nern2015multicolor}. }
        \label{MCFOsystem}
    \end{figure}

    In the GAL4/UAS system, FLP recombinase expression is driven under UAS control and activated by tissue-specific GAL4 drivers. Upon heat-shock induced FLP expression, recombination at FRT sites excises stop cassettes in a random subset of cells, allowing expression of distinct fluorescent proteins from a single transgenic construct.  This results in combinatorial multicolour labeling of individual cells, enabling detailed morphological analysis. Our UAS reporter (smGFP) carried three epitope tags:  HA, FLAG and V5.  

    \subsection{Fly Strains}
    We used \geno{TH-GAL4}, a line that tags all dopaminergic neurons in the central nervous system of the larva \citep{selcho2009thgal4}, and crossed it with \geno{tsh-GAL80} to suppress expression in the VNC. 
    The following driver line was used:  
    \geno{R57C10-FlpL in su(Hw)attP8;; pJFRC210-10XUAS-FRT>STOP>FRT-myr::smGFP-OLLAS in attP2. pJFRC210-10XUAS-FRT>STOP>FRT...}.

    The following effector line was used:   \geno{w; tsh-Gal80/cyo.Tb.RFP; TH-Gal4}.

    These were crossed, and the desired progeny was:  \geno{R57C10-FlpL / + ; tsh-Gal80 / CyO.Tb.RFP ; TH-Gal4 / UAS-smGFP}. To select for larvae that express \geno{GAL4} only in the brain, only larvae with red fluorescent bodies were dissected, as this indicates the presence of \geno{tsh-GAL80}.  
    
    \subsection{Fly preparation}
    On day 1 the flies expressing TH-GAL4 in the brain were crossed with the FLP-containing strain, on molasses plates at 25\degree C. On day 4, the cross was synchronised for 3-4 hours at 25\degree C (the flies were left to lay eggs for 3-4 hrs on a new molasses plate). The plates with freshly laid eggs were then collected and placed at 25\degree C for one day. On day 5, the plates were heat-shocked in the incubator at 37\degree C for 30 minutes.

    Alternative heat-shock regimes were empirically tested (45~min and 1~h at 37\degree C). Both longer treatments produced excessive FLP-mediated recombination, resulting in over-labeling such that many neurons shared the same colour and individual morphologies could not be reliably distinguished. Therefore, a 30~min heat shock at 37\degree C was used for all experiments to balance labelling sparsity and signal strength.

    Finally, on day 6 larval CNSs were dissected. 

    \subsection{Immunohistochemistry}
        For Immunohistochemistry, we adapted the protocol from HHMI Janelia Research Campus, in combination with the protocol from \citep{nern2015multicolor}. The following primary antibodies were used: \textbf{Mouse $\alpha$-Neuroglian; Rabbit $\alpha$-HA Tag; Rat $\alpha$-FLAG Tag} (Sigma). The following secondary antibodies were used: \textbf{AF488 Donkey $\alpha$-Mouse; DL549 Goat $\alpha$-Rabbit; AF594 Donkey $\alpha$-Rat (Sigma)}. The following conjugated antibody was used: \textbf{AF647 Mouse $\alpha$-V5}. 

        Optimization of the protocol required extensive iteration over many months, during which multiple combinations of primary, secondary, and conjugated antibodies were systematically tested. The aim was to obtain strong, specific signals in all imaging channels without cross-reactivity or bleed-through. The final antibody set reported here represents the outcome of this process and was selected because it consistently provided clear labeling across epitopes, enabling reliable multichannel visualization.

        The larval central nervous system~(CNS) was dissected in cold 1$\times$ phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The tissue was then transferred into 2~mL Protein LoBind tubes containing cold 4\% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 1$\times$ PBS, and incubated for 1~h at room temperature (RT) while nutating. The PFA was then removed and tissues were washed in 1.75~mL of 1\% PBT (PBS with 0.3\% Triton X-100) four times for 15~min each with nutation.  
        Samples were then blocked with 5\% Normal Donkey Serum (NDS Jackson Immuno Research; prepared as 95~$\mu$L PBT + 5~$\mu$L NDS), and incubated for 2~h at RT on a rotator with tubes upright.
        Primary antibody incubation was carried out in 1\% PBT (typically 100~$\mu$L per tube) for 4~h at RT, followed by two consecutive overnights at 4$^\circ$C with continuous rotation.  After primary incubation, tissues were washed four times in 1.75~mL of 1\% PBT for 15~min each.  

        Secondary antibody incubation was performed in 1\% PBT (100~$\mu$L per tube) for 4~h at RT, followed by 1--2 overnights at 4$^\circ$C with continuous rotation. Post-secondary washes were performed four times in 1.75~mL of 1\% PBT for 15~min each. An additional blocking step with 5\% Normal Mouse Serum (NMS; Jackson ImmunoResearch, \#015-000-120) in PBT was carried out for 1.5~h at RT prior to overnight incubation with at 4$^\circ$C with the conjugated antibody. Following incubation, samples were washed four times in 1.75~mL of 1\% PBT for 15~min each.  

        For mounting, tissues were placed on poly-L-lysine (PLL)-coated coverslips. Samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30\%, 50\%, 75\%, 95\%, and three changes of 100\%), soaking for 10~min at each step. Tissues were cleared by immersion in three sequential 5~min xylene baths. Finally, samples were embedded by applying 4--5 (80~$\mu$l) drops of dibutyl phthalate in xylene (DPX) to the mounted tissue, placing the coverslip (DPX side down) onto a prepared slide with spacers, and applying gentle pressure to seat the coverslip. Slides were left to dry in the hood for 1--2 days prior to imaging.

    \subsection{Confocal Microscopy}
    Images were acquired on a Zeiss LSM 780 confocal microscope (ZEN) with a Plan‑Apochromat 63×/1.4 oil objective. Laser lines and detection windows were: 488 nm (AF488, 500–550 nm), 561 nm (DL549, 570–595 nm), 594 nm (AF594 Donkey $\alpha$-Rat, 600–640 nm), and 633 nm (AF647, 650–700 nm). To minimize bleed‑through, the 561 nm (DL549) and 594 nm (AF594) channels were acquired sequentially with narrowly spaced emission bands; detector gain/offset were adjusted to avoid saturation.
    Acquisition parameters were chosen to satisfy Nyquist sampling: pixel size ~0.10–0.15 µm (63×), z‑step 0.3–0.5 µm for stacks. Pinhole size was 1 Airy unit, bidirectional scanning enabled, line averaging 2, and scan speed set to minimize photobleaching while preserving signal-to-noise.

    Raw data were saved as .czi files and exported as 16‑bit TIFFs for analysis. Image processing (cropping, linear brightness/contrast adjustments, maximum‑intensity projections, and scale bar annotation) was performed in FIJI/ImageJ \citep{schindelin2012fiji}. All processing preserved linear intensity relationships and was applied equally across comparative image sets.

    \subsection{Matching Confocal Images with Seymour Data} 
    
    To identify neurons imaged with confocal microscopy, I compared the fluorescent images with the EM volume of the first instar larval brain.
    The samples selected for search had to have clearly distinguishable single neurons in one of the channels. Morphological aspects of neurons such as dendritic or axonal processes played a key role in determining the identity of a single cell. Candidate matches in the EM volume were first shortlisted by soma position and gross projection target, then validated by agreement in: (i) trajectory through major neural tracts, (ii) branching order and topology, (iii) arborization domains relative to anatomical landmarks and (iv) axon–dendrite polarity.  
    
    The background staining of neuroglian facilitated visualization of neurite bundles and served as a reference framework. Key anatomical landmarks, including the mushroom body and dopaminergic neuron clusters such as the PPL cluster, were used to align and match confocal images with corresponding structures in the EM volume. Although the mushroom body DANs represented unintended background labeling, they proved useful for establishing spatial correspondence between the two imaging modalities. 

    When a stained neuron from a sample appeared to match more than one candidate in the EM volume (typically no more than two), both candidates were annotated as the same DAN identity with an additional distinguishing label appended (e.g., eDAN-4m and eDAN-4l). This naming convention preserved the inferred cell-type correspondence while acknowledging ambiguity in one-to-one matching.

\section{Loss-of-Function Behavioural Assays}

    \subsection{Fly strains}
    The following effector lines were used: UAS-impTNT-HA, UAS-TNT-HA, obtained from the Bloomington Stock Center (Indiana, USA).

    We used the following split-GAL4 GMR driver lines were used: SS01978, SS26146, SS2856, SS22965, SS04497, SS25740, SS27197, SS36847, SS55970 (HHMI Janelia Research Campus; \citep{li2014gal4, meissner2025splitgal4}). 
    
    \subsection{Behavioural Experiments}

    9 split GAL4 lines were used and crossed with either UAS-TNT (effector) or UAS-impTNT (control) genetic driver lines lines.
    The cross was set at 25$^o$C and the flies were laid on fly food (molasses) for 3 days. The plates were switched on the 4th day, and larvae were then synchronised for 6-8 hours at 25$^o$C. Thereafter, larvae containing the UAS-TNT or UAS-impTNT transgene were raised at 18$^o$C for 7 days with normal cornmeal food. Then, synchronised third instar larvae were collected. 
    Larvae were separated from food by using 15\% sucrose and washed with water, then dried and placed in the center of the arena, consisting in 3\% Bacto agar gel in a 25 × 25 cm square plastic plate.
    Experiments were conducted at 25$^o$C.
    Larvae were monitored with the Multi-Worm Tracker (MWT) software (http://sourceforge.net/projects/mwt); \citep{Ohyama2013PlosONE}.

    For light-stimulation experiments, approximately 30 larvae were assayed per run. Green light was presented for 20~s, and the proportion of larvae turning was monitored before, during, and after stimulation. Six runs were performed for each line.

    \subsection{Behavioural Quantification}
    Larvae were tracked in real-time using the MWT software \citep{Swierczek2011MWT}.  Objects that were tracked for less than 5 seconds or moved less than one larval body length were rejected. For each larva, MWT returns a contour, spine and center of mass as a function of time. From the MWT tracking data we computed the key parameters of larval motion, using Choreography software \citep{Swierczek2011MWT} with variables as in \citep{Ohyama2013PlosONE}. From the tracking data, we quantified crawling and rolling events and the speed of peristaltic crawling strides. %this is wrong? we only checked turning 

    \subsection{Behavioural Data Analysis}
    The data was generated using the LarvaTagger software \citep{laurent2024larvatagger}, via automatic tagging of individual behaviours. 

    LarvaTagger employs a self-supervised autoencoder pre-trained on extensive behavioural datasets from Janelia Research Campus \citep{jovanic2016competitive, masson2020identifying, vogelstein2014discovery}, mapping short behavioural sequences into a low-dimensional latent representation.
    Data were further processed and visualised using \textit{pandas} \citep{mckinney2011pandas}, \textit{matplotlib} \citep{hunter2007matplotlib}, and \textit{seaborn} \citep{waskom2021seaborn} in Python.
    For each animal, turning rate was quantified across three epochs: 20~s before stimulation (baseline), 20~s during green-light stimulation, and 40~s after stimulation (post-stimulus). Turning rates were compared between larvae expressing tetanus toxin (TNT) in the targeted neuron and their control counterparts expressing the inactive form (impTNT). Roll events occurred sporadically across epochs and were not separately analysed.

\section{Ca\texorpdfstring{$^{2+}$}{2+}Imaging with Light Sheet Microscopy}
    \subsection{Fly strains} %RH5 
    The following lines were crossed:
    \geno{w; If/CyO; 57C10-GAL4 in attP2; 20XUAS-IVS-NES-jRGECO1a-p10 in VK00005/TM6B(3)} and \geno{w; 20XUAS-H2B::miRFP703-sv40 attP40; TM6B/MKRS}. The desired progeny (\geno{w; UAS-H2B::miRFP703; 57C10-GAL4, 20XUAS-IVS-NES-jRGECO1a/TM6B}) carried the pan-neuronal driver 57C10-GAL4, the calcium indicator UAS-jRGECO1a, and the nuclear fluorescent marker UAS-H2B::miRFP703. This combination enabled the visualization of calcium activity across the entire larval brain while enhancing the visibility of cell nuclei, thereby facilitating the identification of cellular positions and overall brain structure during imaging.

    This line was subsequently crossed with \geno{y[1] w[*]; P{w[+mC]=Rh5-EGFP.P}2/CyO; MKRS/TM2} to fluorescently label Rh5 photoreceptors. The inclusion of Rh5-EGFP allowed the photoreceptors, which are located outside the larval brain, to be visualized during dissection. Because these structures are highly susceptible to mechanical damage, ensuring their visibility was essential for confirming their presence and integrity. The final progeny used for imaging experiments was \geno{w; UAS-H2B::miRFP703 / Rh5-EGFP; 57C10-GAL4, UAS-jRGECO1a/ MKRS}.


    \subsection{Sample Preparation}
    Fly larvae were raised on standard cornmeal-based food.
    For live imaging, second instar larvae were isolated and dissected in physiological saline. Each larva was pinned with the dorsal side up in a Sylgard-lined Petri dish, and the head was cut at approximately two-thirds of the body length using micro-scissors. The body wall was pinned and its internal organs were removed, and the CNS was carefully dissected free while preserving the fluorescently labelled Rh5 photoreceptors.

    The samples were then embedded in 1\% low-melting-point agarose dissolved in physiological saline at 36\degree C. The agarose-embedded CNS was aspirated into a glass capillary (1.4\,mm inner diameter, 2.0\,mm outer diameter), where it rapidly cooled to room temperature and solidified into a soft gel. The agarose cylinder was subsequently extruded from the capillary, rendering the CNS optically accessible for imaging.


    \subsection{LSM and Functional Imaging}
    Imaging was performed on a dual‑view SiMView light‑sheet microscope at 2.87 Hz with a voxel sampling of 0.406 × 0.406 × 1.7 µm. The SiMView software was used to localize and center the brain, and the two opposing views (anterior/posterior) were oriented to be as flat and central as possible relative to the detection axes.
    Cytosolic jRGECO1a was excited at 561 nm to report calcium dynamics, while nuclear H2B::miRFP703 was excited at 690 nm to visualize cell positions. Rh5 photoreceptors were stimulated with 488 nm light introduced via a dichroic beam splitter \citep{randel2025combining}. The two views were temporally interleaved during acquisition and subsequently registered, deconvolved, and fused using a custom pipeline following established multi‑view procedures \citep{lemon2015whole, tomer2012quantitative}.

    \paragraph{Visual stimulation protocol}
    Rh5 photoreceptors were driven with 488\,nm blue light delivered as 5\,s pulses. Each recording began with a 20\,s baseline, followed by repeated 5\,s ON epochs separated by 60\,s dark intervals (3 repetitions per animal). Across runs, the 488\,nm excitation power was \emph{titrated} in ascending blocks (e.g., 0.02–0.10\% in L\_1\_1, 0.12–0.20\% in L\_1\_2, 0.06–0.14\% in L\_5\_1), and separate runs tested 1\,s vs 30\,s stimuli at 0.10\% power (L\_1\_3, L\_5\_2). 

    
    \subsection{Data Analysis}

    Calcium imaging data were analysed in Python using custom scripts developed in Jupyter Notebook. Fluorescence traces were extracted from defined neuropil regions of interest (ROIs) corresponding to the mushroom body (MB), optic lobe (OL), and protocerebral bridge (PB) in both hemispheres. Stimulus timing was read from the per-run metadata as frame indices for light \emph{ON} (\texttt{onTimes}; 5\,s) and \emph{OFF} (\texttt{offTimes}; 60\,s). The temporal resolution of imaging (\emph{seconds per volume}) was estimated by comparing the number of acquired volumes during ON and dark intervals.

    For each stimulation event (“pulse”),  window of interest comprised the \textbf{10\,s} immediately preceding ON time and the subsequent \textbf{40\,s} after. A baseline fluorescence $F_{0}$ was computed \emph{per event and per ROI} as the mean over the 10\,s pre-stimulus window; normalized activity was then expressed as $\Delta F/F_{0} = (F - F_{0})/F_{0}$. Trials were aligned to stimulus ON ($t=0$\,s) and, for each ROI, I computed the \textbf{mean} $\Delta F/F_{0}$ across stimulations at each time point; dispersion is shown as the \textbf{standard error of the mean (SEM)}. 

    \textit{Numpy} \citep{unpingco2021numpy} and \textit{matbplotlib} \citep{hunter2007matplotlib}, libraries were used to compute plots in Python. 
    In each plot, the visual stimulus epoch is indicated by a transparent blue rectangle from $t=0$ to the \emph{median ON duration} (median of \texttt{offTimes}$-$\texttt{onTimes}, converted to seconds). Averaging and statistics were performed \emph{within a single run} (cross-run pooling was avoided because ROI identities can differ between runs). Figures were generated with \textit{matplotlib} and exported as SVG.
